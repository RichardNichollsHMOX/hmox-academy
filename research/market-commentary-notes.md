# E. Richard Nicholls – Facebook commentary on the HMO market (2018–2026)

Source: two Facebook archive exports (his own posts and group posts/comments). His views only; no people, addresses or personal content. Dates are posting dates; quotes lightly trimmed.

## 1. Themes (dated points, oldest first)

### Yields and pricing
- 2020-07-03: His regional gross yield map for HMO sales: 15%+ low-demand areas; 13–15% secondary northern towns; 12–13% larger cities; 11–12% higher-value cities; about 10% very high demand (Article 4, prime student); 8–10% home counties, Bristol, red-brick university towns; under 8% London and the South Coast. Condition and occupancy can move a property one band. A flat 10% for every HMO is rarely right.
- 2022-09-16: Rent rises do not automatically raise value. If the yield stays fixed and rents rise 15%, the buyer has to put in far more cash above what the lender will lend, so the deal stops working.
- 2022-11-08 / 2022-12-03: After the mini-budget, add about 1% to northern C4 yields. Example: 11% in Feb 2022, 12% in Nov 2022, value flat because rents rose.
- 2024-11-01 / 2024-11-24: Gross yield is "the conclusion" of a net calculation; same-rent HMOs can rightly be 13% and 15.6% if one costs more to run. He holds net yield (about 8.5%) fixed.
- 2025-09-06: Revisited the 2020 map: October 2022 was "the turning point"; yields up almost everywhere. Flat-rent HMOs lose value; with rent growth, £30k at 12.5% (£240k, 2021) becomes £37k at 15% (£246k, 2025). "Net is king."
- 2026-05-06: The average yield at exchange across recent HMO X sales was about 13%, with some sub-10% sales in the South.
- 2026-09-15: "Most HMOs need to hit at least 12% gross yield" to work as an investment. Banks may lend at an effective 10–11% on large Sui Generis schemes, but the resale market trades "a couple of % behind".
- **How his view changed:** in 2020 he gave fixed regional bands, with some compression by 2021. From late 2022 he says yields must rise, and that rent growth is the only thing protecting value.

### Buyer types (Buyer A / Buyer B)
- 2022-01-06: Ready-made HMO buyers are often cash buyers from the South or overseas who are busy in other careers. "Where a developer sees opportunity, these buyers see risk."
- 2022-09-03: Buyer A is the person who buys a C3 house and creates value. Buyer B has decided not to develop or add value, and will pay a premium for a well-run, well-papered HMO.
- 2024-11-30: In 2023/24 there were more A buyers than B buyers, as squeezed margins pushed people into adding value. Many B buyers left because management failed to deliver the promised hands-off returns; some moved to social housing, serviced accommodation or commercial.
- 2025-12-13: Funds mainly buy student, supported living and social housing assets; traditional HMO portfolios sell to family offices, wealthy individuals, small funds and operators.

### Supply and oversupply
- 2020-10-23: Non-Article 4 northern towns can be a "Wild West" of packaged deals. Room rates there are often £80–£95 a week, and only a few locations can sustain £120+.
- 2024-07-10: "HMOs are the most transient property type"; most 2024 sales were "premature".
- 2025-07-31: Most new HMOs aim for the top of the rental pyramid. As newer stock arrives, most settle into the middle tiers.
- 2026-03-31: "The riskiest BRRR" (buy, refurbish, refinance, rent) is the boom in secondary towns near big cities. There are only so many tenants there who can afford premium rents.

### Article 4 and planning
- 2024-04-14: Values slide with oversupply and through the 18–24 months to enforcement as conversions rush in; afterwards they stabilise and usually recover to "before the slide". A small, sought-after zone adds more value than a wide one.
- 2024-11-03: "Article 4 is your friend not your foe" for buyers of ready-made HMOs.
- 2026-06-09: No national Article 4: good for BRRR investors, but it continues "the postcode lottery".
- **How his view changed:** in 2020, non-Article 4 areas were a risky free-for-all. By 2024–26, Article 4 was a protection for established stock and a value driver.

### Licensing and compliance
- 2021-11-10: Minimum for a yield price: licensed and compliant, planning or lawful use, good occupancy record, furniture included, quality managing agent, passed inspection.
- 2025-06-19: Fire risk assessments (FRAs), asbestos reports and lawful development certificates are "slowing down an already tediously slow conveyance".
- 2026-04-26: Non-compliant HMOs are increasingly heading for vacant possession sales at a discount. "No sophisticated investor will pay a premium to inherit someone else's compliance exposure."

### Renters' Rights Act (RRA)
- 2026-04-25: "Not the end of HMOs. But it is the end of winging it." HMO tenants turn over naturally, so losing Section 21 matters less unless it was a crutch. Selling with vacant possession needs four months' notice (Ground 1A). One rent increase a year, open to challenge, so "room rents have to be set right from the beginning". Buyers will scrutinise tenancy paperwork more.
- 2026-05-10: "Not all HMOs lose under the RRA." As standards tighten, quality stands out. "It's a market reset."

### Lending and interest rates
- 2020-07-13: After the first lockdown, funds and cash buyers carried the market as mortgaged buyers hit nil and down-valuations.
- 2022-10-07: Most buyers who need UK lending were "out of the game". He advised targeting overseas, cash and bulk buyers and expecting low offers.
- 2025-02-06: Valued a new 7-bed below the bank's £550k: with £42.5k stamp duty a buyer's ROI was about 8%, so £500k was needed to sell.
- 2025-02-02: Higher stamp duty since October 2024 hurt saleability; company (SPV) sales became popular but carry risks and costs.
- 2026-03-17: Swap rates jumped about 0.5% in days. "Volatility — not collapse." If a deal still works at a 6%+ stress rate, it is probably solid.

### Refinancing and leverage
- 2020-08-05 / 2020-10-24: Many sellers came to sell only after a refinance valued too low. A sale can reach a yield price that a refinance could not.
- 2022-01-21: Treat a sale as plan B: "If it values up as expected, keep it. If you can't get the refinance you hoped for, sell it."
- 2025-04-04: Over-leveraging can create "a debt-piled ponzi"; developers are moving to commercial conversions as 6-bed all-money-out BRRRs stop working.
- 2025-10-29: Negative equity often comes from "the rent roll piss take": leveraging on day-one rents that later fall back.
- 2026-01-27: Refinanced at 10% on £850 rooms; market trades at 12–13% on £725 rooms: about £435k exit value against a £475k loan.
- 2026-03-10 / 2026-05-17: Stress-test bills, rents and rates over 3–5 years, and leverage against a "blended sustainable rent roll". "Taking a loan out based off 115% of the long term blended rental value will do serious damage."
- 2026-08-07: The equity taken out at the first refinance "is the ONLY big chunk of equity that this HMO will ever produce". "You've already eaten your cake."

### Management
- 2020-07-21: "Occupancy is the only thing that matters." Know your tenant base.
- 2020-08-16: Judge a managing agent by visiting their houses, not by occupancy figures. A 95%-full house can still be badly run.
- 2025-05-07: "A lone wolf, old school, 'sod it' style HMO landlord is not a good place to be any more." He called it the age of systems and structure.
- 2026-08-11: "Why take on commercial-grade lending to then operate your asset in an amateur-grade way?"

### Valuation method and comparables
- 2021-11-10: For 5–6 bed C4 HMOs, a buyer will pay up to about £30–50k above bricks and mortar (the lender level). Beyond that is "nose bleed territory".
- 2022-11-01: Getting the "HMO premium" usually means a cash top-up above the lending level. That might be 5–10% for an outstanding HMO and nothing for a poor one.
- 2024-03-14: Some HMOs are valued as bricks and mortar plus a modest premium (£140k house, £160k as an HMO).
- 2025-01-06 / 2026-02-05: The student yield trap. Student comparables (7–8%) reflect high house values, lower rents and bills not included. A professional HMO's equivalent yield is about 4–6% higher.
- 2025-09-29: "I've had a down valuation" is "very rarely true". Usually a lender's valuer simply did not reach the borrower's hopes.
- 2026-01-04: "Exit comps ultimately dictate finance values." A bad sale creates a bad comparable for others.

### Auctions
- 2025-06-17: Launched an HMO-only auction house; "bidding wars are rare now".
- 2025-09-18: Reported a 78.6% sold rate after the latest auction cycle.
- 2026-05-06: Average exchange yield of about 13%. "A keen guide brings the bidders. Bidders bring competition. Competition lifts the price."

### Selling well
- 2021-11-15: Be honest with tenants about the sale. There are "5 or 6 chips of credit" with them for visits, so plan for 3–4 investor viewings.
- 2022-05-04: HMO portfolios sell for "the sum of the parts", unlike BTL (buy-to-let) portfolios, where buyers expect a bulk discount.
- 2025-06-19 / 2025-08-22: "HMOs either need to be a 'deal' or 'ideal' to sell well in this market." He called it a two-tier market.
- 2025-12-13: Expect the sum of the parts for portfolios, not yield compression. There is a sweet spot at 6–10 HMOs.
- 2026-06-06: Fall-throughs were cut by checking compliance early and making sure buyers had the right lending.
- **How his view changed:** in 2022 portfolios simply achieved the sum of the parts. By 2025 he was warning against the "scale and exit to a fund at 6%" belief.

### Mistakes investors make
- 2019-11-19: Packaged deals assuming £125 a room a week where reality is £80–90, and commercial valuations that never come.
- 2020-02-28: Judging an HMO at purchase or refinance instead of on long-term performance. "Net income is all that counts."
- 2022-03-30: Signing a rent-to-rent (R2R) contract. "When a R2R contract is active on your HMO, your HMO is only worth 'bricks and mortar'."
- 2026-04-22: Creating "an unsaleable asset" by never asking who will buy it in 5–10 years.
- 2026-05-28: The leverage trap, and assuming a new HMO stays at the top tier ("Tier 1"). Most good en-suite HMOs are "Tier 2 assets over the long term".

### Where the market is heading
- 2022-11-08: HMOs would be more resilient than wider residential property because they produce income and have many buyer types.
- 2026-02-11: In his poll, 43% planned to keep developing, 21% to buy established stock, 21% to consolidate and 14% to exit.
- 2026-07-29: The cost of building a like-for-like HMO now often exceeds the price of a ready-made one, so the established resale market offers value.

## 3. Durable lessons for a general course
- Judge an HMO by its long-term net income and occupancy, not by launch-day rents or how it looks.
- Gross yield is shorthand. Buyers and valuers work back from a net return, so higher running costs mean a higher yield and a lower price.
- Rent growth protects value when yields rise. With flat rents and rising yields, values fall.
- There are two broad buyer groups: those who add value (A) and those who want a finished, compliant, well-managed product (B). Know which one your HMO suits.
- A premium above bricks and mortar is earned through licensing, planning or lawful use, a strong occupancy record, good management and clean paperwork. It has limits, and usually needs the buyer to add cash above what the lender will lend.
- A refinance has one valuer. A sale reaches many buyer types. Plan a sale route even if you mean to hold.
- Leverage against a blended, sustainable rent roll, and stress-test bills, rents and interest rates.
- The big equity release happens once, at the first refinance. Do not plan on a second.
- Oversupply in a local market can wipe out rent growth. Build for a real, sustainable tenant base.
- Article 4 tends to cause a dip during the run-up, then protect established HMOs with lawful use.
- Be honest with tenants about a sale and keep viewings to a minimum.
- Compliance gaps (licence, FRA, EICR, lawful use) slow or kill sales and push HMOs towards discounted vacant possession sales.
- Under the Renters' Rights Act, well-run HMOs with proper tenancies are least affected. Set rents correctly from the start.
- Portfolios usually sell for the sum of the parts, not at a premium for scale.
- In today's market an HMO sells well if it is either a clear "deal" or an "ideal" turnkey asset.
